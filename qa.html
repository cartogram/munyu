Questions

The questions we expect after the talk. Each has a short answer, then a fuller one.

“Doesn’t this just hand more power to whoever owns the AI?”

A generated UI can be empathetic to you — or to whoever owns it.

There’s a plausible future where your agent generates the interface and you never see the underlying app — the “buy” button drawn for you by the platform, tuned to its goals, not yours. Same technology, pointed the opposite way. The capability that could give Sarah an interface shaped around her could give a platform one shaped around its incentives. The deciding factor is who the UI is empathetic to — which is exactly why local, user-owned generation matters.

“If everyone’s UI is different, don’t we lose common ground?”

Real cost. Keep the periphery visible — personal shouldn’t mean sealed.

Yes — you and I could buy the same thing and never know, because our interfaces showed it differently. And a UI that only shows what it thinks you need is a filter bubble: you stop stumbling on the useful thing you weren’t looking for. Honest answer: it’s a real cost, and the mitigation is to keep peripheral information visible — a personal UI shouldn’t be a sealed one. Worth naming rather than pretending personalization is free.

“Will people actually use this, or just accept the default?”

The empathy framing sidesteps it — the default already fits.

People still use untitled.txt over more powerful tools because customizing has an upfront cost — “I’m busy, I just need to finish.” So will users shape these interfaces or just accept what the AI hands them? We don’t fully know. But empathy largely sidesteps it: the whole point is the default already fits, so there’s little to tinker with. An empathetic interface does the accommodating — the user shouldn’t have to. That’s the opposite of untitled.txt, which does nothing until you do everything.

“An interface that knows me that well — what about my privacy?”

A real risk. Local models may keep that context on your own device.

Everything we’ve proposed depends on the interface knowing your context: your task, your energy budget, that you steer a wheelchair with your head. The empathetic half — the “who is doing this” — needs to know the most about you.

So privacy is an issue. Local models could get us a long way: if the model runs on your own device and the context never leaves your machine, personalization stops being a privacy trade-off. Local models are improving quickly, so we’re hopeful rather than certain.

“But a UI that keeps changing breaks screen readers.”

Right: stability is a basic trait of accessibility and of good design in general. Our answer is memory, and least surprise is actually empathy applied. So hopefully this corrects itself with empathetic interfaces.

The sharpest objection: if interfaces are generated, don’t you destroy what a screen-reader user relies on — the button being where it was yesterday? Stability is a basic trait of accessibility, and of good design in general. A constantly re-rolling UI would be a disaster.

Our answer is memory. The interface is generated once, for you, then remembered and reused. And an empathetic interface applies exactly this: the least surprise. It knows you navigate by memory, so it holds still — personalize the content, not the structure. So we hope the problem corrects itself: an interface that understands you wouldn’t move your controls.

Today’s one-size-fits-all UI is the volatile one: it changes under you whenever a vendor redesigns it — that’s the Ravelry change that put Rose in bed for three days.

“Does this actually work today?”

Not quite. Models can generate working interfaces; the plumbing around them still fails.

Be honest about where we are. Eight months after our interviews, Rose tried to build a bespoke Steam library manager for herself. It didn’t really work out: lack of support, lack of knowledge, still too time-consuming, too much tinkering. Rose is technical and motivated — if anyone could build her own tool, it’s her. We replaced configuration toil with developer toil: the AI generated a front end, and what defeated her was the glue — storage, accounts, the plumbing you don’t know exists unless you write software.

Is that failure telling us the idea is wrong, or that we’re just early? We think it’s the moment — and we have evidence, not just hope. Google Research found that generating a working interface is an emergent capability: their newest model produced valid UIs with zero errors, while a model one generation older failed on 60% of prompts. It crossed a threshold rather than improving gradually.

So our honest position: the plumbing that beat Rose is a property of today’s tooling, not of the idea. We could be wrong — but we think it’s a matter of time, and not much of it.
