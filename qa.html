Questions

Six interviews — isn’t that too few to conclude anything?

Too few to measure anything, enough to test a hypothesis. They’re why ours changed.

We didn’t set out to measure anything. We had a hypothesis — that chatbots and agents are a revolution for accessibility — and we wanted to test it against real people’s days rather than our own assumptions. Too often people talk about people with disabilities, not with them.

Six long conversations, recruited through Fable, were enough to break that hypothesis. Every one of them had adapted their setup, and every one of them still ran into its limits. That pattern, not a statistic, is what the rest of the talk builds on.

Slides: Hypothesis · Interviews

Isn’t this just better settings and personalization?

No. Settings bend one interface toward you; we’re saying start from you.

Settings, plugins and assistive technology are adaptation: layers stacked on one interface built for the average person. That model got us a long way — watching Myron work, the barriers can get remarkably low. But every layer adds complexity. Sarah’s bold-reading plugin broke her voice control, and her grid overlay vanished under her fake dark mode.

And configuring costs energy, the budget Amelia and Rose spend fighting apps instead of using them. Most people never configure anything; they just live with the default. So we flip it: the person comes first, and the interface is generated from them. The default already fits, so there’s nothing left to configure.

Slides: Today’s model · One UI doesn’t fit everyone

Your interviewees want assistants, not replacements. Isn’t a generated UI the AI taking over?

The AI shapes the interface; you still do the task.

That was the clearest thing we heard. Michael would rather do twenty deterministic clicks than let an agent send things with his name on them. Amelia said struggling through things is part of what makes life enjoyable. Nobody wanted the task taken away.

A generated interface doesn’t take it away. It changes how you do the work, not who does it: Myron still decides what goes in the spreadsheet, he just says it in one command instead of thirty grid steps. What goes away is the disability tax, the extra work Amelia pays before she gets to the part she actually cares about.

Slides: No revolution · One task, many UIs

So should I stop worrying about accessibility in my app?

No. Generated interfaces are built from what you ship today.

Our interviewees use today’s software today, and it has to work for them now. Standards and best practices are what made Myron’s setup possible at all.

And the shift we describe doesn’t replace that work, it builds on it. An AI that maps your app onto a fresh interface goes through what your app exposes: its APIs, its structure, its semantics. The better those are, the better what it can generate. Apps that are well built and well described are the ones a person’s own interface can use.

Slides: Today’s model · The economics changed

Does this actually work today?

Partly. Rose built her own app, but it took her a lot of time. It’s the worst it will ever be.

Eight months after our first interview, Rose had built an app to organize her Steam library. It picks the game that fits her energy budget at that moment, and she keeps changing it as suits her. That’s the idea working, for one person, today.

It still took her a lot of time, and Rose is technical: she studies cyber security. Most people couldn’t do what she did yet. But the models are moving fast. Google Research found that generating a working interface is an emergent capability: their newest model produced valid interfaces for every prompt, while a model one generation older failed on 60% of them. It crossed a threshold rather than improving gradually.

So we don’t think the remaining effort is a property of the idea, just of today’s tools. We could be wrong, but we think it’s a matter of time.

How do you see the implementation of this taking effect?

We wanted to focus on the people, not the technology. And you can essentially do this today, by asking Claude.

This talk is about the people we interviewed and what they need, so we deliberately didn’t design an architecture for it. How it gets built matters less to us than who it gets built around.

And you don’t have to wait for one. You can essentially do this today: tell Claude what you’re trying to do and what it should know about you, and ask it to build you the interface. That’s what an artifact is, a small app made on the spot for one task, and it can take the empathy half into account as soon as you give it that context.

An interface that knows me that well — what about my privacy?

A real risk. Local models may keep that context on your own device.

The empathy half needs to know the most about you: your condition, where you are, how much energy you have today, that you steer a wheelchair with your head. Sarah was most impressed by AI when it knew more about her brain injury than she did, and that is exactly the kind of knowledge you wouldn’t want to leave your hands.

Local models could get us a long way. If the model runs on your own device and the context never leaves it, personalization stops being a privacy trade-off. Small apps like Rose’s can already live on the device with local data. Local models are improving quickly, so we’re hopeful rather than certain.

Slides: Sarah · Imagine everything is bespoke

Doesn’t a UI that keeps changing break screen readers?

Right: stability is basic to accessibility. An interface that understands you holds still.

The sharpest objection: if interfaces are generated, don’t you destroy what a screen reader user relies on, the button being where it was yesterday? Molly runs her screen reader fast because she knows her setup; Myron has memorised where things sit on his screen. A constantly re-rolling interface would be a disaster for both.

Our answer is memory. The interface is generated for you, then remembered and reused. And an empathetic interface applies least surprise: it knows you navigate by memory, so it holds still. Personalize the content, not the structure.

It’s today’s one-size-fits-all interface that changes under you, whenever its vendor redesigns it. That’s the Ravelry redesign that put Rose in bed for three days.

Slides: Rose · Imagine everything is bespoke · Source: Ravelry

If everyone’s UI is different, don’t we lose common ground?

A real cost. Personal shouldn’t mean sealed, and software can still be shared.

Yes. If interfaces shape how we think, what happens when we no longer share a visual language? You and I could use the same service and never be able to help each other with it. Rose already lives a small version of this: nobody in her family can use anyone else’s iPad.

We don’t think it has to be all or nothing. Software that only one person uses is hard to justify for long, so we expect it to spread to friends, families and communities who build and share apps among themselves, like Maggie Appleton’s barefoot developers. And a personal interface shouldn’t be a sealed one: it can keep the periphery visible, so you still stumble on the thing you weren’t looking for.

Slides: By the people, for the people · Imagine everything is bespoke · Source: Appleton, 2024

Doesn’t this make us all more dependent on AI, and on whoever owns it?

Yes, and we don’t know where that leads. A generated UI is empathetic to whoever owns it.

This is the drawback we’re least sure about. AI keeps becoming a resource-hungry utility we all depend on, and an interface generated for you needs a model to generate it.

Ownership matters too. The capability that could give Sarah an interface shaped around her could give a platform one shaped around its incentives: the “buy” button drawn for you, tuned to its goals, not yours. Same technology, pointed the other way. That is why we keep coming back to models that run on your own device, and to people building software for themselves, like Rose.

What we do know is that personalized interfaces are happening now, for the first time. So now is when to talk about these problems and address them.
