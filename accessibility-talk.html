Your UI is not my UI

Simon Lenz, Matthew Seccafien

Every person who uses software arrives with a different combination of needs, abilities, devices, and context. The current model of accessibility — adapting a single interface through layered tools and settings — has been valuable, but it puts the burden on the user to configure and maintain a setup that works. We believe AI opens a fundamentally different path: generating interfaces that are personal from the start. This talk brings real user research, a working prototype, and a provocation about what the future of UI could look like.

Hypothesis

Chatbots and agents are a total revolution for accessibility

We think there must be something else.

Our hypothesis: one very simple and accessible interface (natural language chat) replaces all previous interactions. We are not at the mercy of benevolent app/website developers creating UIs compatible with you.

User Interviews

We interviewed six people with disabilities

In order to prove this hypothesis we conducted 6 user interviews, with real users who struggle daily with a physical or cognitive disability. We talked about their daily struggles, setups and AI usage.

For accessibility there’s often a tendency to talk about rather than with people with disabilities. We wanted their perspectives front and center.

No Revolution

On agents & chatbots

  • They are very helpful tools
  • Simple interface is very accessible
  • Trust & Reliability is a large problem
  • We want assistants and not replacements
This is the punchline of section 2: chatbots and agents are useful, but they are not a replacement for purpose-built interfaces. That takes us into the limits of the current adaptive model.

Today's model: one interface, layered settings

  • Screen readers
  • Reduced motion
  • Dark mode
  • Zoom
  • Larger text
Section 3 (~3 min). This is today's accessibility model: a single interface, adapted through layered tools and settings. It works — up to a point. Our interviews revealed where it breaks down: conflicting adaptations, exhausting configuration, and the gap between what standards provide and what individuals actually need.

Opportunities

  • Setups are very personalized
  • Adaptations conflict with each other
  • Configuration costs energy
  • Standardization has limits

What if launching an app means creating an app?

Section 5 (~5 min). We broaden the scope beyond disability. Everyone arrives with different needs, situations, and abilities. What would it mean for browsers, apps, and platforms if interfaces were generated rather than served?

Focus shifts from UI to API

  • Launching an app is programming an app
  • Focus on content and functionality (APIs), not UI
  • The interface becomes disposable — generated fresh, every time

Applications become art, not commodity

  • A hand-crafted UI stops being the default way software is distributed
  • Disabled users are just the ones suffering most from this today
  • Personalized UIs would help everyone, not only accessibility use cases

Revisiting: 8 months later

Rose Lorraine tried to build a bespoke Steam library manager

Section 6 (~3 min). Our initial research for this started internally, and 8 months later we revisited it in preparation for this talk. Rose Lorraine attempted to build a bespoke manager application for her Steam library.

It didn't really work out

  • Lack of support
  • Lack of knowledge
  • Still too time consuming
  • Too much tinkering required

We replaced configuration toil with developer toil.

Will this get better over time?

Probably — today's tinkering reflects immature tooling.

Tentative lean: this friction looks like a snapshot of where AI dev tooling is today, not a permanent property of personalized/generated interfaces. Expect it to shrink as the tools mature. Still an open question worth sitting with before section 7.

The Future.

Section 7 (~3 min). We explain how the industry is showing key indicators that prove the potential in our new thesis, and segway into Q&A.

Omarchy

DHH's agentic Linux distro reduces the desktop to a terminal-first, AI-agent-driven workflow — the OS itself becomes conversational.

Google Search

AI Mode assembles custom layouts on the fly — interactive visuals, tables, graphs, simulations — generated per query, not served from a template.

Artifacts

Every major AI harness now treats “produce a working interface, inline, on demand” as a core, indispensable feature — not a novelty.

Personalized interfaces are inevitable

It's in the best interest of these products to be empathetic to the person in front of them — and that makes personalization a leap the industry is already taking.

This leads to discussions for the community to think about beyond this talk: what does this mean for how we build and distribute software?

Key takeaways

  • Why the “single adaptive interface” has fundamental limits
  • What real users with disabilities say about AI, agents, and control
  • A concrete demonstration of AI-generated personalized interfaces
  • A framework for thinking about the shift from adaptability to personalization
  • Open questions about what this means for how we build and distribute software
