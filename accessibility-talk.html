Your UI is not my UI

Simon Lenz, Matthew Seccafien

Every person who uses software arrives with a different combination of needs, abilities, devices, and context. The current model of accessibility — adapting a single interface through layered tools and settings — has been valuable, but it puts the burden on the user to configure and maintain a setup that works. We believe AI opens a fundamentally different path: generating interfaces that are personal from the start. This talk brings real user research, a working prototype, and a provocation about what the future of UI could look like.

Hypothesis

Chatbots and agents are a total revolution for accessibility

We think there must be something else though.

Our hypothesis: one very simple and accessible interface (natural language chat) replaces all previous interactions. Users are not at the mercy of benevolent app/website developers creating UIs compatible with them anymore.

User Interviews

We interviewed six people with disabilities

In order to prove this hypothesis we conducted 6 user interviews, with real users who struggle daily with a physical or cognitive disability. We talked about their daily struggles, their setups and AI usage.

For accessibility there’s often a tendency to talk about rather than with people with disabilities. We wanted their perspectives front and center.

Melody

AI as an Accessibility “Assistant,” not a Replacement

“I do use AI, but only as an assistant for what I'm looking for. Not to do the searching from scratch.”

Melody struggled to see and uses a collection of tools to navigate her life relatively independently.

Uses a screen reader at a very fast speed, and a tool called Be My Eyes when necessary.

Likes AI for specific things, not for the whole task — she felt she was faster and better herself, and preferred that sense of autonomy.

She felt AI wins on speed, humans win on trust.

Matthew

Accessibility AI should be like training wheels, not autopilot

“[…] you have to put your name to things. If it went ahead, did a bunch of tasks, emailed people, and I couldn't really take it back — that feels … uncomfortable, giving it that much autonomy.”

Matthew is autistic and has ADHD, and also struggles with active listening due to tinnitus.

Very specific about what he required or wanted — this changed depending on the situation.

He sometimes preferred granular control, such as for anything law-related, and other times was fine using AI.

He wasn't lazy — he'd choose 20 complicated clicks over an unpredictable text prompt, as long as it was reliable and deterministic.

Sinead

AI can explain the cause, not just adapt the interface

“I was really struggling one day … but then, interestingly, it came up with a really insightful reason: it was the drop in barometric pressure. The change had been so fast. And it said that's really common with people with brain injuries.”

Sinead came to disability later in life — she had a brain injury five years ago. As a result screen time is difficult for her, and reading long texts or a lot in one day becomes really hard.

Her newness to having a disability showed in our interview: she struggled more than the others we spoke to. For example, a Chrome plugin that bolded half of each word conflicted with another plugin that overlaid numbers on elements for voice control — so she'd overlay a grid of numbers instead, but the grid lines were dark and didn't show up with her dark-mode plugin enabled. Her whole setup doesn't work in her browser of choice, Safari, and breaks down entirely if she wants to watch a lecture at the same time.

She had strong preferences for making tools work for her — like preferring Gwyneth Paltrow's voice in her screen reader — but struggled to get these varying extensions to work well together.

AI providing information in an interface that is highly compatible and tailored for the user — not just adapting a layout, but making sense of what's happening to them.

No Revolution

  • Agents & chatbots are very helpful tools
  • Simple interface is very accessible
  • Trust & Reliability is a large problem
  • We want assistants and not replacements

At this point in the interviews we put our heads together for an intermediate result: agents and bots are very helpful tools, but no revolution yet.

Reasons why not: there's a large overhead for the general population, not just an accessibility-specific one. Trust and reliability are big hindrances.

People want tools and assistants, not to be replaced — they want to stay in the driver's seat and “do the work” themselves. And this is the key insight we carry forward: the answer isn't AI doing the whole task for you — that strips out the autonomy and trust these users told us they need. The better move is AI that produces an interface which lets you do the task yourself, more easily.

Chatbots and agents are useful tools, but they are not a replacement for purpose-built interfaces.

So we continued, to see what else is out there.

Myron

AI can provide an interface where you are already a power user

“If I had an AI box, I could tell it to line up all my headers from cell A1 to A20 and just send it. That would save so much time. That would be a life changer for me.”

Myron is a college professor in Louisiana who has written a book. No perception problems — it's purely about movement, from a spine injury 23 years ago.

He's very experienced with voice control — steers his wheelchair with head movement, and in Bluetooth mode the same controls also drive the mouse pointer on his screen. In the screenshot he's reading out grid numbers (“7, 8, 9, 7”) to click precisely.

Takeaway: he's an expert user of his setup, but it took him 23 years to get there. Can AI help shorten that path for others?

Today's model

Today's accessibility model: Vendors create a single interface. Users need to live with this interface or use adapation layers stacked on top. Those tools (like screenreaders, reduced motion, dark mode, zoom or font size increases) rely on the original application being written in a compatible way according to standards.

It works — up to a point. Our interviews revealed where it breaks down: conflicting adaptations, exhausting configuration, and the gap between what standards provide and what individuals actually need.

Opportunities

  • Setups are very personalized
  • Adaptations conflict with each other
  • Configuration costs energy
  • Standardization has limits

Let's brush aside all the “normal” AI problems, like reliability — others will solve that, and it's the same problem for disabled and non-disabled users alike.

Instead, we saw a lot of friction specifically in traditional accessibility areas: setups are very personalized and hard to master, independent tools and adaptations conflict with each other, and standardization has limits — one interface can't fit the range of needs we saw across just six people.

So for the next half of our talk, we want to propose a new concept…

Your UI is not my UI

Section 4 (~3 min). Our core proposal: AI-generated bespoke interfaces. Instead of adapting one UI for everyone, generate a unique UI for each person.

Your UI is not my UI

Personalization over adaptability

We're proposing this because of what the research forced on us. Everyone we spoke to had done real work to adapt a standard interface — and for every one of them it kept failing: Sinead's plugins fought each other, Rose's family needs incompatible settings, Allana burns her energy budget on uncooperative apps.

Adaptation assumes one interface underneath that everyone bends toward themselves. That's where it breaks. So the question isn't “how do we adapt better?” — it's “what if the interface were personal from the start?” A UI that is the result of two things: what you're trying to do, and who you are. In one word, the interface becomes empathetic to the person in front of it — it meets you where you are, so the reconciling work disappears. That empathy is what makes this an accessibility argument, not just a generation demo.

Your UI is not my UI

Create specific UIs on the fly for your needs

  • Adapt to your current task
  • Adapt to your preferences
  • Adapt to your accessibility needs
  • Adapt to your circumstances
  • Adapt to your state of mind
  • Adapt to your external environment

Where we see this going: launching an app is creating an app, just for you, in that moment. Instead of layering tools on top of a UI to adapt it to your needs, the UI would be generated from scratch, on the fly, and disposed of when you're done with it.

We all come into an interface as slightly different people every time, and this newer way of thinking about interfaces can empathize with that — tailoring itself to your task, time of day, location, stress level, atmospheric conditions, and more. The same app for one user, or even the same user, might look different depending on these and many other conditions.

We're predicting the “look and feel” of our products becomes infinitely fluid. We either tell it explicitly, or it knows intuitively.

And “telling it explicitly” no longer means learning the app's settings. The instruction becomes plain language — you describe what you need instead of hunting through menus to configure it. Adaptation was something you configured; personalization is something you can just ask for.

What if launching an app means creating an app?

Section 5 (~5 min). We broaden the scope beyond disability. Everyone arrives with different needs, situations, and abilities. What would it mean for browsers, apps, and platforms if interfaces were generated rather than served?

Focus shifts from UI to API

  • Launching an app is programming an app
  • Focus on content and functionality (APIs), not UI
  • The interface becomes disposable — generated fresh, every time

One way to say what changes: we stop designing screens and start designing outcomes. The question flips from “what should this screen look like?” to “what is this person trying to do right now?” The layout is downstream of intent — and once the system optimizes for the outcome rather than the clicks, the interface stops being the fixed thing and the goal becomes the fixed thing.

There's a second version of this worth naming: instead of the app generating a UI for you, the app just exposes its functionality — an API — and you bring your own agent or interaction layer to drive it.

For our interviewees this isn't hypothetical. Myron already brings his own interaction layer — head-tracking and voice control that drive any application. When the app exposes a clean API rather than a fixed UI, it can finally meet Myron through the modality that already fits his body, and give Matthew the reliable, legible interaction he needs — the interface bends to the person instead of the person bending to the interface.

Applications become art, not commodity

  • A hand-crafted UI stops being the default way software is distributed
  • Disabled users are just the ones suffering most from this today
  • Personalized UIs would help everyone, not only accessibility use cases

Why have we always had one-size-fits-all interfaces? It's not a design failure — it's economics. Building a custom app for every individual need was never affordable, so we abstract what a big population has in common and ship one reusable solution. That's why there's no dinner-party app, no family-travel app, no grant-writing app — everyone does those differently. Instead we get rigid apps (the developer decides what matters — Airbnb won't show “pet-friendly” on the map) or bloated ones (everything dumped in). AI changes that economics: for the first time, the made-for-one interface is affordable. That's the “why now.”

And it's not a new idea — the field's founders chased it for decades. Engelbart, in the 1960s, complained that we design for a “statistical population” with no attempt to fit the individual. David Karger later put it: “no developer can predict all the ways a user will want to manipulate information.” They were right, and stuck — the idea was lovely but impractical, because ordinary people had no way to build it. That's the part that just changed.

Google Research frames the shift well: LLMs already turned a finite library of texts into an infinite one — generated on the spot for any need. Interfaces are next: instead of picking from a finite library of apps, you get the right interface generated on the spot, for you.

For accessibility this lands hardest. No standards body can anticipate Sinead's exact combination of a brain injury, dark-mode plugin, and voice-control grid. The person who understands that interface best is Sinead — and for the first time, it can be made for her, in her context.

Revisiting: 8 months later

Rose tried to build a bespoke Steam library manager

Section 6 (~3 min). Our initial research for this started internally, and 8 months later we revisited it in preparation for this talk. Rose attempted to build a bespoke manager application for her Steam library.

It didn't really work out

  • Lack of support
  • Lack of knowledge
  • Still too time consuming
  • Too much tinkering required

We replaced configuration toil with developer toil.

Let's not rush past this, because it's the most honest moment in the talk. The thing we just spent ten minutes proposing — a bespoke, personal interface — failed for the exact person we proposed it for. Rose is technical, motivated, and disabled in a way that makes off-the-shelf software genuinely painful. If anyone should have been able to build her own tool, it's her. And she couldn't.

We didn't remove the toil — we moved it. Configuration toil became developer toil. The AI happily generated a front-end; what defeated her was the glue — storage, accounts, the plumbing you don't even know exists unless you write software. (Maggie Appleton, who evangelizes this exact future, has hit the same wall building her own apps. It's not just us.)

So we have to hold both things at once: the vision is real, and today it doesn't work for the people who need it. Sit in that for a beat before we ask whether it gets better — because the answer only means something if we've been honest about the failure first.

Is Rose's failure the idea, or the moment?

We think it's the moment.

Here's the real question that failure poses: is it telling us the idea is wrong, or that we're just early? We think it's the moment, not the idea — but we're saying that carefully, because it's easy to wave away a failure with optimism you haven't earned.

The one piece of hard evidence: Google Research found that generating a full working interface is an emergent capability — their newest model produced valid UIs with zero output errors, while a model one generation older failed on 60% of prompts. It didn't improve gradually; it crossed a threshold. What defeated Rose a year ago is exactly the kind of thing that goes from “impossible” to “routine” in one model release.

So our honest position: the plumbing that beat Rose is a property of today's tooling, not of the idea. We could be wrong. But the trend line, and the requirement from our users, both point the same way.

Whose context is it?

  • A UI generated for you needs to know a lot about you
  • For our interviewees that's the most sensitive data there is — health, disability, cognitive state
  • Generated interfaces only work if that context can stay private
  • On-device / local AI is the path that keeps it that way

There's an obvious tension we have to name. Everything we've proposed depends on the interface knowing your context — your task, your energy budget, that you have a headache from a pressure drop, that you steer a wheelchair with your head. That's exactly the data you'd least want leaving your device.

This is where local-first and on-device models matter beyond just being technically neat. If the model runs locally and the context stays on your machine, personalization stops being a privacy trade-off. Apple's recent direction — run most AI on-device, only reach for the cloud when you truly need the extra compute — is roughly the shape we'd want.

So the promise isn't just “your UI adapts to you.” It's “your UI adapts to you without handing your most personal data to a server you don't control.” For the users we spoke to, that's not a nice-to-have — it's the difference between a tool they can trust and one they can't.

Q&A prep — the darker version of this: a generated interface can be empathetic to you, or empathetic to whoever owns it. There's a plausible future where your agent generates the interface and you never see the underlying app — the “buy” button is drawn for you by the platform, tuned to its goals, not yours. That's the same technology pointed the opposite way. So be honest: the capability that could give Sinead an interface shaped around her could also give a platform an interface shaped around its incentives. The deciding factor is who the UI is being empathetic to — which is exactly why local, user-owned generation matters.

Q&A prep — the personal-silo risk. If everyone's UI is different, we lose common ground: you and I could buy the same thing and never know, because our interfaces showed it differently. And a UI that only ever shows you what it thinks you need is a filter bubble — you stop stumbling on the useful thing you weren't looking for. Honest answer: this is a real cost, and the mitigation is to keep the periphery visible — a personal UI shouldn't mean a sealed one. Worth naming rather than pretending personalization is free.

Q&A prep — the “good enough” adoption doubt. People still use untitled.txt over more powerful tools because customizing has an upfront cost and “I'm busy, I just need to finish the thing.” So will users actually shape these interfaces, or just accept whatever the AI hands them? We don't fully know. But the empathy framing largely sidesteps it: the whole point is that the default already fits, so there's little to tinker with. An empathetic interface does the accommodating — the user shouldn't have to. That's the opposite of untitled.txt, which does nothing until you do everything.

“But a UI that keeps changing breaks screen readers.”

Personal is not the same as volatile

  • Screen readers and muscle memory depend on stability
  • Personalize the content and what's pre-selected — never relocate the controls
  • Generated once for you — then remembered and reused
  • Stability is empathy for anyone who navigates by memory

This is the sharpest objection, and we should meet it head-on — it's the question an accessibility audience will ask. If interfaces are generated fresh every time, don't you destroy the one thing a screen-reader or keyboard user relies on? Consistency. Muscle memory. The button being where it was yesterday. A constantly re-rolling UI would be an accessibility disaster.

The answer is that personalization and volatility are not the same thing. “Generated for you” does not have to mean “regenerated from scratch each time.” The right model is memory: the interface is generated once, for you, and then remembered — pinned, stable, reused. And this is itself empathy: an interface that truly understands a screen-reader user understands that they navigate by memory, so it holds still. Contrast today's one-size-fits-all UI, which changes underneath you whenever a vendor ships a redesign (remember Rose and the Ravelry change that put her in bed for three days). A personal, remembered UI is arguably more stable, because it's shaped around a person who needs it to stay put.

The sharp version of the rule: personalize the content, not the structure. Don't move the button — change what's shown and what's already selected. Same layout, personalized state. That's the version a screen-reader user can trust: not “different every time,” but “different for each person, then dependable for that person.”

The Future.

Section 7 (~3 min). We explain how the industry is showing key indicators that prove the potential in our new thesis, and segway into Q&A.

The industry is already doing this

  • Google Search AI Mode — custom layouts assembled per query, not served from a template
  • Artifacts — every major AI tool now generates working interfaces inline, on demand

We're not out on a limb here. The generate-the-interface capability is already shipping in the products you use every day — Google Search assembling a custom layout per query, every major AI tool producing working interfaces inline. The engine exists and it's being built at enormous scale.

But notice what these systems take into account: your intent — the query, the task in front of you. That half is already handled. The half that isn't, yet, is the human: your needs, your abilities, your context. Today's generated UI answers “what are you trying to do?” but not “who is doing it?” That missing half is our whole argument.

Key takeaways

  • Today we adapt one majority interface — it can't serve the variety of humans
  • An interface should account for both the task and the human doing it
  • With generated UIs, that's economically viable for the first time in history

If you take one thing: the generated-interface future is coming either way — it's already shipping, and Google Research measured people preferring generated interfaces over the usual chat “wall of text” in 83–93% of cases. So this isn't a maybe.

Our contribution is the requirement — that it be empathetic to the person in front of it, which is what the accessibility community has needed all along. That's the difference between an interface that helps you and one that just does the task.
