Your UI is not my UI

We talk to six people with unique daily struggles
and run into the hard limits of adapting a single interface. Using AI we could unlock an infinite number of interfaces,
generated with empathy to our individual personal situations,
capabilities and needs.

    Staff Developer—DeepL

Simon

Hello everyone, my name is Simon. I live in Germany and I’m a software engineer with an interest in accessibility.

Matt

Hi, I’m Matt, I am a Canadian, living for the past 6 years in Berlin. We both work at DeepL and we’re lucky to be part of the accessibility chapter there.

We're a self-organized group engaging with the accessibility of our AI translation and voice products, and of course, their users.

Earlier this year at our company kick-off we gave a lightning talk. It was well received and the result is what started to become this presentation.

When we started out though, we only knew it was going to be on AI & Accessibility, and we'd figure the rest out along the way.

Chatbots and agents are a revolution for accessibility

Simon

So, to give our talk direction, we decided to come up with a hypothesis:

Chatbots and agents are a revolution for accessibility.

Our thought was that: One very simple, and therefore accessible, interface — the natural language chat of these tools — replaces all previous interactions.

No more struggling through websites, apps and content with patchy accessibility support. Actually you can hand over the whole task to the AI, instead of struggling through yourself.

In short: chatbots and agents would remove most of the small and large obstacles people run into every day while relying on screen readers or other assistive technology.

User
research

Matt

In order to prove this hypothesis we conducted 6 interviews, with real users who struggle daily with a physical or cognitive disability. We talked about their daily struggles, their setups and how they use AI in their day to day or work.

Interviews

We talked with (not about) six people who have a daily physical or cognitive disability and asked them about their setups and their AI use.

Matt

We notice a tendency to talk about, rather than with, people with disabilities, and given we had the means (through our resources within the accessibility chapter) we wanted their perspectives front and centre.

We used Fable to facilitate these interviews which keeps their contact details confidential. All names have been changed for this presentation.

Molly

Uses AI as an assistant, not a replacement.

“I do use AI, but only as an assistant for what I’m looking for. Not to do the searching from scratch.”

Matt

So first, we met Molly. She struggled to see and uses a collection of tools to navigate her life relatively independently. She uses a screen reader at like 4x speed and appeared very capable. But was also interesting to hear that she relies on humans through a tool called Be My Eyes when necessary.

This connects her to volunteers when she needs additional support through their app. She had started using AI for some things, but failed to get it to match the speed and reliability of just doing things herself.

Michael

Uses AI as training wheels, not autopilot.

“[…] you have to put your name to things. If it went ahead, did a bunch of tasks, emailed people, and I couldn’t really take it back — that feels … uncomfortable, giving it that much autonomy.”

Matt

Next we spoke with Michael. He is autistic, has ADHD, and tinnitus, which made listening very difficult for him.

Whether or not he reaches for AI in his day depended a lot on what he was doing because he liked being in control. If he was working on something pertaining to laws, he couldn't get over having to trust the agent and would prefer doing the 20 complicated, deterministic clicks himself.

Sarah

With context about you, AI can help you in ways you didn’t anticipate.

“I was really struggling one day … but then, interestingly, it came up with a really insightful reason: it was the drop in barometric pressure. The change had been so fast. And it said that’s really common with people with brain injuries.”

Matt

Next was Sarah. She was located in Canada so we immediately connected! Unfortunately, she had a brain injury five years ago, so came to disability very late in life and struggled more than Molly and Michael. Screen time was hard; reading a lot in one day is hard; watching lectures (she was studying for a sailing degree) was also hard.

Her whole setup felt like fight. She had a chrome plugin that would bold half of every word to make it easier for her to read, but this would break her voice-over software because it treated each bolded segment as something she could click. Because of this, her whole screen would just fill with numbers overtop of everything. So she switched to the grid overlay version of this plugin, so the clickable regions appear as grid quadrants over the screen that she would use to progressively narrow in on the element, but then the grid lines would be impossible to see once she enabled "darkify" which was a chrome plugin to give a faux dark mode (the university platform she was using didn't support it on its own). And sometimes she would have to switch to Safari for certain tasks, and the whole fragile set up would collapse if she had to watch a lecture video or, God forbid, join a video call.

It was also interesting to hear about how she listened better when it was Gwyneth Paltrow’s voice, so she had a interest in fine control of her tools, but couldn't figure it out. She actually wanted AI to help her, and was most impressed when it demonstrated that it knew about her illness and situation, even more than she did.

In the quote she describes a conversation where the agent pointed out the recent weather as a culprit for a particularly difficult day.

No revolution

  • Chatbots & agents: helpful tools
  • The simple interface is very accessible
  • Trust & reliability are the problem
  • We want assistants, not replacements

Simon

At this point in the interviews we regrouped to form an intermediate result. Which was that agents and bots are very helpful tools, but no revolution yet.

The reasons overlap with the general population. They are not accessibility-specific.

Trust and reliability are big hindrances.

People want tools and assistants, they don't want to be replaced. They want to stay in the driver’s seat and “do the work” themselves. Because in the end they are responsible. But also because it is fulfilling.

So we continued, to see what else is out there.

Amelia

AI should remove the disability tax on everyday tasks.

“Part of the enjoyable thing in life is struggling through things. Having everything taken care of for you takes away a bit of the fun.”

Simon

Amelia studies law in the UK. Her issues are with reading and writing; she struggles with high contrast and bright interfaces. Her computer is set to a large zoom level and she uses a forced dark mode plugin.

A big theme with her was Energy budget. And lot of her budget is spent fighting apps that don’t cooperate with her setup and behave unpredictably.

Besides that she emphasized that she actually wouldn’t want everything taken care of for her — she just wants AI to remove that extra tax she has to pay because of her disability.

Rose

Everyone’s settings are different and incompatible.

“I live in a disabled house […] all our settings are different, even across our iPads. It’s actually an in-house joke: I can’t look at my husband’s iPad, he can’t look at mine, and the kids hate using either.”

Simon

Rose studies cyber security. She has hyperphotosensitivity — she lives with closed curtains and wears very dark sunglasses outside. Because light drains her energy and can genuinely make her sick.

She shared one striking example: a design change on Ravelry (a knitting website she uses), that once put her in bed for three days. Because it was jarringly not compatible with her setup and immediate drained her energy budget..

Next to energy budget, we also talked about how highly personalized her setup is. It even changes quite a bit throughout the day.

Myron

An interface where you’re already a power user

“If I had an AI box, I could tell it to line up all my headers from cell A1 to A20 and just send it. That would save so much time. That would be a life changer for me.”

Simon

Our last interview was with Myron, who is a college professor from Louisiana. He has no perception problems — for him it’s purely about movement. He had a spine injury 23 years ago, which left him paralyzed only with with head movement.

He steers his wheelchair with head movements, and in Bluetooth mode the same controls also move his mouse pointer.

Watching him work was impressive. He’s very experienced with Dragon NaturallySpeaking for voice control: he knows its shortcuts, makes his own and has memorised where things sit on his screen. To click, he uses the “show grid” command: then nine numbered squares overlay the screen, and each number he says zooms into that specific square, now with a smaller grid. So a sequence like “7, 8, 9, 7” moves the cursor to the precise location of the icon he intented to click.

What slows him down is the number of small steps in one task. Each is its own command: “Show grid. 7, 8, 3. Click. Scroll down. Show grid …”, through every menu. Imagine working on a complicated Excel sheet, this can get tiresome quickly.

Ideally he’d just say what he wants done, and have it done in one go.

Matt

All our interviewees had adapted their setups according to their personal needs. And all of them ran into issues. That takes us to, the limits of adaptability.

The limits of
adaptability

Matt

Today’s model

One interface for the average, with tools and settings on top

…and they start to conflict

Matt

In Today’s model we ship one interface. Users have to either live with it or stack adaptation layers on top.

We saw in the interviews how Sarah's various assistive technologies and configurations (voice over, dark mode, bionic reading) began to conflict with each other. Each one of them requires that the app was built in a compatible way with standards and best practises in mind.

That approach brought us quite far. It’s amazing to see in the interviews how low the barriers can get. But we also noticed its limitations. Each cumulative layer adds more complexity to the app, and more potential for conflict. And more energy spent to maintain it. And more potential for errors.

One interface can’t fit everyone

  • Setups are very personal
  • Adaptations conflict with each other
  • Configuration costs energy
  • Standards have limits

Matt

This one interface trying to fit the unique needs of its users, results in a unique number of errors. Everyone we spoke to had done real work to adapt and configure a standard interface, and for every one of them it kept failing. Either their setup was so personal, their adaotions were incompatible, configurations were too granular, or they just lived with the broken experience.

And as we were talking about this, we started talking about what would be a better approach in today's world.

Personalization over adaptability

Matt

Adaptation assumes one interface underneath that everyone bends toward themselves, but that’s where it breaks.

So instead of improving how we adapt to the standard interface, we want to propose that the interface is focused more on this personalization from the start.

With today’s AI, this becomes a possibility.

A UI generated for your needs.current task.preferences.accessibility needs.circumstances.state of mind.external environment.needs.

  • your current task
  • your preferences
  • your accessibility needs
  • your circumstances
  • your state of mind
  • your external environment

Matt

A UI should be the result of two things:

  • what you’re trying to do,
  • and who you are, in that moment

Start clicking through the rotor

An app for the exact same task may need to look different depending on your needs, preferences, accessibility needs, circumstances, state of mind external environment and so on.

Empathy is
our point

Simon

We call this empathy.

In one word, the interface needs to be empathetic to the person in front of it.

UI = f(Intent, Empathy)

Intent What you’re trying to do

Empathy Who you are right now

Simon

So for us, a UI should be a function of two elements.

The intent: what you’re trying to do, which was the the same box for everyone in the example. And the empathy: who you are, your preferences and needs in this very moment.

And from the equation follows directly: Same intent, different person, should result in a different UI.

UI = f(Intent, Empathy)

Simon

Generated UIs are already emerging everywhere around us. But so far they only take into account your intent, the query, the task.

So that half is handled. The half that isn’t, yet, is the human: your needs, abilities, context.

That missing half is our whole argument. We think there is lots of missed opportunity here. For accessibility but also outside of this specific topic.

Source: Google Research, 2025

Your UI is not my UI

Matt

Generated on the fly and fully functioning, but also ephemeral and purpose built. You use it to do your task, but I might do the same task with a UI for me, and both are discarded when we are done. There is less linking your UI to my UI. Or even my UI versus my UI tomorrow given the same task.

One task, many UIs

Simon

Before we move on, I'd like to reiterate what we've said so far, now on a concrete example.

Next Let's remember Rose from our interviews. Suppose she wants to translate some text into another language.

In our previous model we started with the interface and the person had to adapt. In the new model, we start with the person.

Next ×3 Then what we know about her, one box at a time:

  • her condition: hyperphotosensitivity. Light drains her energy and can make her sick, so nothing on screen may be bright;
  • her environment: on the couch, curtains drawn, so the room is dark and any glare stands out;
  • her situation: a medium energy budget today, so the UI should ask as little of her as it can.

Next And only now, creates AI the interface. Base on all those inputs above. The result is a dark, low-glare translator on her iPad.

NextLet's now switch to Myron, who arrives with the same intent (to translate text), but in a vastly different context.

Next ×3

  • His condition: a spine injury, 23 years ago. He can’t use his hands and steers his wheelchair and his mouse pointer with his head;
  • his environment: at his desk, headset on, so his voice is always within reach;
  • his situation: focused on a spreadsheet, so nothing should pull his head, and his pointer, away from it.

Next Based on this input, the AI-generated UI is focused on speech input. It looks vastly different to Rose's to the left, even though they started with the same task.

Next And it doesn’t stop at those two examples two. Suppose someone who maybe needs reading glasses or nothing at all you'd naively call a disability, but is in a crowded subway with one hand free, running late in a heatwave.

Next Their UI would look different again, because their needs are different: big, clear buttons where the thumb reaches, readable without the glasses they didn’t bring.

Next And this list goes on: Human conditions, situations and contexts are basically indefinite, and the UI should take that into account.

UI = f(Intent, Empathy)

  • Rose No redesign shocks,
    no clash of settings
  • Myron Higher-order commands,
    voice-native UI
  • Sarah No stack of
    incompatible plugins

No adaptation necessary anymore.

Simon

What is gained by the empathy half? Let’s go back to the people we met.

  • Rose doesn’t need to fear that a sudden redesign makes an app unusable for her, and her family’s settings no longer clash,
  • Myron doesn’t need to steer the OS’s voice control grid one tiny step at a time, but can issue higher level commands for the whole task &
  • Sarah doesn’t need a stack of plugins fighting each other.

Users are not at the mercy of apps being compatible with their adaptations anymore.

Actually, adaptation is no longer necessary at all, instead the app is built on the spot, bespoke for that user, and their specific and momentary needs.

Launching
is creating

Matt

Another way of thinking about this is: What if launching an app is creating an app.

Apps can’t predict you

“No developer can predict all the ways a user will want to manipulate information.”
David Karger

Not just how you use them, but who you are.

Matt

The problem is that apps can’t predict you even if they tried.

This isn’t a new insight. We make things for the statistical average, most common person. We couldn’t possibly predict all the ways a user will want to interact with your app, and we cover what we can.

And it’s not only how users interact. It’s also the needs, how you feel, where you are, your preferences right now all make you highly unpredictable. No vendor can account for that.

Source: Karger et al., 2005

The economics changed

“We are excited about a future where users don’t have to pick from a finite library of applications or visual pages, but instead, they have access to an infinite catalog, where the right ephemeral interface is generated on the spot tailored for their need.”
Google Research

Matt

Bespoke apps were never economically viable. It would be too expensive. Therefore the app had to make a choice for you and take a stance.

Google Maps lets you avoid tolls, highways, and ferries, but not winding roads, if they make you carsick.

AI could change this. Apps become APIs, and the UIs are not of high concern because they are part of the LLM.

The functionality already exists as APIs; what AI makes cheap is the interface on top. For the first time, the single-serving interface is possible or affordable. Google Research frames the shift well by saying LLMs already turned a finite library of texts into an infinite one, and it going to continue the same way with these application and tailored visual interfaces.

As it happens, when we talked with Rose again, one year later, to see how things had progressed since our first interview, she had created an app to organize her Steam library. It would give her a recommendation that best respected to her energy budget at that moment. She had the power to create this herself exactly how she wanted it, make edits overtime and while it still takes a huge investment of time and energy and maintenance and might not always produce a lasting result, the whole thing is only possible because the economics have changed, and only getting faster and better.

Source: Leviathan et al., 2026

What if launching an app means creating an app?

We started with disability.

But everyone benefits from bespoke software.

Matt

Back to our hypothesis. Is AI a game changer? We think so, but not in the way we originally thought.

It’s not that the AI is an assistant that takes away the task as a whole.

After our research, we think what’s truly remarkable is that AI makes task-driven software development accessible to everyone. The effort involved with generating an application for you on the spot is feasible. We can create apps from our highly individual little lives, interfaces to accommodate our different needs, situations and abilities. And in certain contexts we think this will be a net benefit.

Imagine everything is bespoke

  • Privacy
  • Stability
  • Isolating

Matt

But obviously a whole boat load of new problems on such topics as:

  • Privacy
    An interface made for you, needs to know a lot about you.
  • Stability
    How do you get predictable interfaces if they are made from scratch every time?
  • Interfaces shape how we think, so what happens to the common ground between humans if we don’t share a common interface.

And of course, AI continues to become the utility we all depend on. Probably not good, we don’t know, but what we do know is that now with AI, personalized interfaces are for the first time actually happening. So we’re in a place where we can actually think about and address those issues.

Closing

Simon

Before we close, let's reiterate what we've discussed so far…

Key takeaway

So far, we ship one interface and users have to adapt.

Ideally, the interface is built for the task and the human.

Today, AI makes those bespoke interfaces viable for the first time.

Simon

So far, the answer to human variety is adaptation: we ship one interface, built for the average. It can’t fit everyone, and the burden of making it work lands on the user.

Ideally, the interface is built for the task and the human in front of it. Generated interfaces are already happening, but they start from the task alone. We argue they should take the person into account too: UI is a function of intent and empathy.

And that ideal isn’t out of reach anymore. Building a bespoke interface for every person and every moment used to be far too expensive. Today’s AI makes it possible for the first time.

For accessibility, that’s a paradigm shift: the burden moves from the user to the interface. But it doesn’t stop at disability. We all have needs, contexts and bad days, so everyone benefits from software that fits them.

Back to Sarah

No adapting, no fighting plugins.

Her focus stays on the lecture.

Simon

Let’s end where we started — with a person, not a paradigm. Remember Sarah, her plugins getting in each other’s way, and a setup that fell apart whenever she wanted to watch a lecture.

Now imagine the interface arrived already understanding her: what she’s trying to do, and who she is that day. She no longer has to worry about adapting it, and can focus on what she came for: the lecture itself. For Sarah, that’s the difference between software she can use and software she can’t.

Q&A

“Doesn’t this just hand more power to whoever owns the AI?”

A generated UI can be empathetic to you — or to whoever owns it.

There’s a plausible future where your agent generates the interface and you never see the underlying app — the “buy” button drawn for you by the platform, tuned to its goals, not yours. Same technology, pointed the opposite way. The capability that could give Sarah an interface shaped around her could give a platform one shaped around its incentives. The deciding factor is who the UI is empathetic to — which is exactly why local, user-owned generation matters.

“If everyone’s UI is different, don’t we lose common ground?”

Real cost. Keep the periphery visible — personal shouldn’t mean sealed.

Yes — you and I could buy the same thing and never know, because our interfaces showed it differently. And a UI that only shows what it thinks you need is a filter bubble: you stop stumbling on the useful thing you weren’t looking for. Honest answer: it’s a real cost, and the mitigation is to keep peripheral information visible — a personal UI shouldn’t be a sealed one. Worth naming rather than pretending personalization is free.

“Will people actually use this, or just accept the default?”

The empathy framing sidesteps it — the default already fits.

People still use untitled.txt over more powerful tools because customizing has an upfront cost — “I’m busy, I just need to finish.” So will users shape these interfaces or just accept what the AI hands them? We don’t fully know. But empathy largely sidesteps it: the whole point is the default already fits, so there’s little to tinker with. An empathetic interface does the accommodating — the user shouldn’t have to. That’s the opposite of untitled.txt, which does nothing until you do everything.

“An interface that knows me that well — what about my privacy?”

A real risk. Local models may keep that context on your own device.

Everything we’ve proposed depends on the interface knowing your context: your task, your energy budget, that you steer a wheelchair with your head. The empathetic half — the “who is doing this” — needs to know the most about you.

So privacy is an issue. Local models could get us a long way: if the model runs on your own device and the context never leaves your machine, personalization stops being a privacy trade-off. Local models are improving quickly, so we’re hopeful rather than certain.

“But a UI that keeps changing breaks screen readers.”

Right: stability is a basic trait of accessibility and of good design in general. Our answer is memory, and least surprise is actually empathy applied. So hopefully this corrects itself with empathetic interfaces.

The sharpest objection: if interfaces are generated, don’t you destroy what a screen-reader user relies on — the button being where it was yesterday? Stability is a basic trait of accessibility, and of good design in general. A constantly re-rolling UI would be a disaster.

Our answer is memory. The interface is generated once, for you, then remembered and reused. And an empathetic interface applies exactly this: the least surprise. It knows you navigate by memory, so it holds still — personalize the content, not the structure. So we hope the problem corrects itself: an interface that understands you wouldn’t move your controls.

Today’s one-size-fits-all UI is the volatile one: it changes under you whenever a vendor redesigns it — that’s the Ravelry change that put Rose in bed for three days.

“Does this actually work today?”

Not quite. Models can generate working interfaces; the plumbing around them still fails.

Be honest about where we are. Eight months after our interviews, Rose tried to build a bespoke Steam library manager for herself. It didn’t really work out: lack of support, lack of knowledge, still too time-consuming, too much tinkering. Rose is technical and motivated — if anyone could build her own tool, it’s her. We replaced configuration toil with developer toil: the AI generated a front end, and what defeated her was the glue — storage, accounts, the plumbing you don’t know exists unless you write software.

Is that failure telling us the idea is wrong, or that we’re just early? We think it’s the moment — and we have evidence, not just hope. Google Research found that generating a working interface is an emergent capability: their newest model produced valid UIs with zero errors, while a model one generation older failed on 60% of prompts. It crossed a threshold rather than improving gradually.

So our honest position: the plumbing that beat Rose is a property of today’s tooling, not of the idea. We could be wrong — but we think it’s a matter of time, and not much of it.

Source: Leviathan et al., 2026, section 3.1
