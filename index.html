Chatbots and agents are a revolution for accessibility

So we came up with a hypothesis: chatbots and agents are a revolution for accessibility.

One very simple, and therefore accessible, interface — the natural language chat — replaces all previous interactions. No more struggling through websites, apps and content with patchy accessibility support.

In short: chatbots and agents would remove most of the small and large obstacles people run into every day when they rely on screen readers or other assistive technology.

User
research

Interviews

We interviewed six people with disabilities about their setups and their AI use.

In order to prove this hypothesis we conducted 6 user interviews, with real users who struggle daily with a physical or cognitive disability. We talked about their daily struggles, their setups and AI usage.

For accessibility there’s often a tendency to talk about rather than with people with disabilities. We wanted their perspectives front and center.

Melody

Uses AI as an assistant, not a replacement.

“I do use AI, but only as an assistant for what I'm looking for. Not to do the searching from scratch.”

Melody struggled to see and uses a collection of tools to navigate her life relatively independently.

Uses a screen reader at a very fast speed, and a tool called Be My Eyes when necessary.

Likes AI for specific things, not for the whole task — she felt she was faster and better herself, and preferred that sense of autonomy.

She felt AI wins on speed, humans win on trust.

Matthew

Uses AI as training wheels, not autopilot.

“[…] you have to put your name to things. If it went ahead, did a bunch of tasks, emailed people, and I couldn't really take it back — that feels … uncomfortable, giving it that much autonomy.”

Matthew is autistic, has ADHD, and struggles with active listening due to tinnitus.

He was very specific about what he wanted, and it changed by situation — granular control for anything law-related, fine with AI elsewhere.

Not lazy: he'd choose 20 complicated clicks over an unpredictable prompt, as long as it was reliable and deterministic.

Sinead

With context about you, AI can help you in ways you didn't anticipate.

“I was really struggling one day … but then, interestingly, it came up with a really insightful reason: it was the drop in barometric pressure. The change had been so fast. And it said that's really common with people with brain injuries.”
[Matt puts notes from prague here as they were better]

Sinead had a brain injury five years ago — came to disability later in life. Screen time is hard; reading a lot in one day is hard.

Her setup is a stack of fighting extensions: a plugin that bolds half of each word breaks the one that overlays numbers for voice control; the number-grid alternative is invisible under her dark-mode plugin. It doesn't work in Safari, and it collapses the moment she also wants to watch a lecture.

She has strong preferences (she likes Gwyneth Paltrow's voice in her screen reader) — but can't get these tools to work together.

AI providing information in an interface that is highly compatible and tailored for the user — not just adapting a layout, but making sense of what's happening to them.

foreshadowing: We found her quote interesting, as the AI helped her in unanticipated ways by being empathetic to her condition and environment.

No revolution

  • Chatbots & agents: helpful tools
  • The simple interface is very accessible
  • Trust & reliability are the problem
  • We want assistants, not replacements

At this point in the interviews we put our heads together for an intermediate result: agents and bots are very helpful tools, but no revolution yet.

Among the reasons, there is a large overhead with the general population. They are not accessibility-specific.

Trust and reliability are big hindrances.

People want tools and assistants, not to be replaced. They want to stay in the driver's seat and “do the work” themselves.

So we continued, to see what else is out there.

Allana

AI should remove the disability tax on everyday tasks.

“Part of the enjoyable thing in life is struggling through things. Having everything taken care of for you takes away a bit of the fun.”

Allana studies law in the UK. Her issues are reading and writing; she struggles with high contrast and bright interfaces — her setup is large font and forced dark mode.

Energy budget is the big theme: a lot of hers is spent fighting apps that don't cooperate with her setup and behave unpredictably.

She wouldn't want everything taken care of for her — she just wants AI to remove that extra tax.

Rose

Everyone's settings are different and incompatible.

“I live in a disabled house […] all our settings are different, even across our iPads. It's actually an in-house joke: I can't look at my husband's iPad, he can't look at mine, and the kids hate using either.”

Rose studies cyber security. She has hyperphotosensitivity — she lives with closed curtains and wears very dark sunglasses outside. Light drains her energy and can genuinely make her sick.

Striking example: a design change on Ravelry (a knitting website she uses) once put her in bed for three days.

Her setup is highly personalized and changes quite a bit throughout the day.

Myron

An interface where you're already a power user

“If I had an AI box, I could tell it to line up all my headers from cell A1 to A20 and just send it. That would save so much time. That would be a life changer for me.”

The limits of
adaptability

Today's model

One interface for the average, with tools and settings on top

…and they start to conflict [let's make this present already in the beginning]

Today's model: the vendor ships one interface, built for the statistical average.

Users have to either live with it or stack adaptation layers on top. We saw in the interviews assistive technologies (screen reader, high contrast, magnification) — but also the configuration everyone does for themselves: dark mode, themes, shortcuts, notification settings, extensions.

Those adaptations require that the app was built in a compatible way with standards in mind.

That approach brought us quite far. It's amazing to see in the interviews how low the barriers can get. But we also noticed its limitations.

[Next] We have stacked adapations, none of them knowing about the others, all of them conflicting with each. That's Sinead's setup in one picture.

One interface can't fit everyone

  • Setups are very personal
  • Adaptations conflict with each other
  • Configuration costs energy
  • Standards have limits

One interface just can't fit the wide variety of needs of its users. Everyone we spoke to had done real work to adapt and configure a standard interface — and for every one of them it kept failing: Sinead's plugins fought each other, Rose's family needs incompatible settings, Allana burns her energy budget on uncooperative apps.

Setups are very personal, and standards have their limits: they can only cover what a population has in common.

So we want to propose a new concept…

A new concept

Personalization over adaptability

Adaptation assumes one interface underneath that everyone bends toward themselves. That's where it breaks.

So instead of improving how we adapt to the standard interface, we want to propose that the interface is created personalized for you right from the get go.

With today's AI models, this becomes a possibility.

A UI generated for your needs.current task.preferences.accessibility needs.circumstances.state of mind.external environment.needs.

  • your current task
  • your preferences
  • your accessibility needs
  • your circumstances
  • your state of mind
  • your external environment

A UI should be the result of two things:

  • what you're trying to do,
  • and who you are.

[Here start clicking through the rotor] An app for the exact same task needs to look different depending on the condition, state, environment and preferences of the user. In one word, the interface needs to be empathetic to the person in front of it.

The empathetic UI meets you where you are, so the adaptation work disappears.

Where this goes: launching an app is creating an app, just for you, in that moment. Generated on the fly, instead of adapted with layered tools.

There is less linking your UI to my UI. Or even my UI versus my UI tomorrow.

One task, many UIs

In our previous model we started with the interface and the person had to adapt. In the new model, we start with the person.

[Next] Remember Rose from our interviews. She wants to translate this text.

[Next, three times] Then what we know about her, one box at a time:

  • her environment: on the couch, curtains drawn, so the room is dark and any glare stands out;
  • her situation: a medium energy budget today, so the UI should ask as little of her as it can.

[Next] Only then the UI. It's the result of the intent and the person: a dark, low-glare translator on her iPad.

Empathy is
our point

UI = f(Intent, Empathy)

Intent What you're trying to do

Empathy Who you are right now

That's what the demo just showed, in one line. A UI is a function of two things. The intent: what you're trying to do, the same box for everyone. And the empathy: who you are right now, made of your condition, environment and situation.

Same intent, different person should result in a different UI.

The industry is already doing this

  • Artifacts — AI tools generating working interfaces on demand

Generated UIs are already emerging everywhere around us. But so far they only take into account your intent, the query, the task.

That half is handled. The half that isn't, yet, is the human: your needs, abilities, context.

That missing half is our whole argument. We think there is lots of missed opportunity here.

UIs with empathy

  • Remove the adaptation burden from the user
  • Don't rely on apps built with accessibility in mind
  • Remove compatibility friction between adaptations

What is gained?

  • Rose doesn't need a dark mode plugin nor fear that a sudden redesign of an app makes it unusable for her,
  • Myron doesn't need to rely on the OS's voice control grid &
  • Sinead doesn't need a stack of plugins fighting each other.

Users are not at the mercy of apps being compatible with their adaptations anymore.

Actually, adaptation is no longer necessary at all, instead the app is built bespoke for that user, and their specific and momentary needs.

Another way of thinking about this is: What if launching an app is creating an app.

Launching
is creating

Apps can't predict you

“No developer can predict all the ways a user will want to manipulate information.”
David Karger

Not just how you use them, but who you are.

The problem is that apps can't predict you even if they tried.

This isn't a new insight. We make things for the statistical average, most common person. We couldn't possibly predict all the ways a user will want to interact with your app.

And it's not only how users interact. But also the empathy factor from the last section. Your needs, how you feel, where you are, your preferences right now all make you unpredictable. No vendor can account for that. It would be too expensive.

The economics changed

Google Research: LLMs already turned a finite library of texts into an infinite one — generated on the spot for any need.

Bespoke apps were never economically viable. Therefore the app had to make a choice for you and take a stance.

Google Maps lets you avoid tolls, highways, and ferries, but not winding roads, if they make you carsick.

AI changes this. apps becomes these APIs. The UI is a wave of a magic wand.

AI changes the cost side. The functionality already exists as APIs; what AI makes cheap is the interface on top. For the first time, the made-for-one interface is affordable. Google Research frames the shift well: LLMs already turned a finite library of texts into an infinite one — generated on the spot for any need. Interfaces are next.

As it happens, when we talked with Rose again, to get an update to se how things progressed since our first interview. And she created an app to organizise or stream library according to her preferences. Not only how she wanted to organize games, but bringing to this interface her disability. (Only possible because economics have changed)

What if launching an app means creating an app?

We started with disability.

But everyone benefits from bespoke software.

Back to our hypothesis. Is AI a gamechanger? We think so, but not in the way we originally thought.

It's not that the AI is an assistant that takes away the task as a whole.

After our research, we think what's truly remarkable is that AI makes task-driven software development accessible to everyone. The effort involved with launching an application delivered to you is approaching the same as generating a new one for you on the spot. We now have full control to steer the app to our highly individual little lives, interfaces to accomodate our different needs, situations and abilities. And we all benefit from this.

Imagine everything is bespoke

  • Privacy
  • Stability
  • Isolating

So, what if? You open your laptop and only see bespoke apps and interfaces, everything fitted to your needs.

There are surely open questions around this, on topics like:

  • privacy (an interface made for you, needs to know a lot about you)
  • stability (how do you get predictable interfaces if they are made from scratch every time?)
  • interfaces shape how we think, so what happens to the common ground between humans if we don't share a common interface

We don't know, but what we do know is that now with AI, personalized interfaces are for the first time an actual possibility. So we're in a place where we can actually think about and address those issues.

Closing

Key takeaways

    [can we shorten the bullet points a few words?]
  • One interface can't fit everyone and the burden lands on the user
  • A generated UI should serve both the task and the human
  • With AI, bespoke interfaces become viable for the first time

Today's answer to human variety is adaptation: one interface, built for the average, with tools stacked on top. It can't fit everyone, and the burden of making it work lands on the user.

Generated interfaces are already happening and will become more prevalent. We argue we shouldn't create them from the task alone, but also from the human in front of it, i.e. make them empathetic. That could change the current accessibility paradigm and generally be more useful for everyone.

No fighting plugins. No setup that collapses when she opens a lecture.

Just an interface that already fits her.

Remember Sinead: the bolding plugin fighting the voice-control grid, the grid lines invisible under dark mode, the whole fragile stack collapsing the moment she wants to watch a lecture. Empathetic to her from the first moment. That's not a nice-to-have for Sinead. That's the difference between software she can use and software that puts her in bed for three days.

Backup — Q&A

Anticipated objections

Backup slides for likely questions — not part of the main flow. Jump here if asked about ownership/agency, fragmentation, adoption, privacy, screen readers and stability, or whether this works today.
“Doesn't this just hand more power to whoever owns the AI?”

A generated UI can be empathetic to you — or to whoever owns it.

There's a plausible future where your agent generates the interface and you never see the underlying app — the “buy” button drawn for you by the platform, tuned to its goals, not yours. Same technology, pointed the opposite way. The capability that could give Sinead an interface shaped around her could give a platform one shaped around its incentives. The deciding factor is who the UI is empathetic to — which is exactly why local, user-owned generation matters.

“If everyone's UI is different, don't we lose common ground?”

Real cost. Keep the periphery visible — personal shouldn't mean sealed.

Yes — you and I could buy the same thing and never know, because our interfaces showed it differently. And a UI that only shows what it thinks you need is a filter bubble: you stop stumbling on the useful thing you weren't looking for. Honest answer: it's a real cost, and the mitigation is to keep peripheral information visible — a personal UI shouldn't be a sealed one. Worth naming rather than pretending personalization is free.

“Will people actually use this, or just accept the default?”

The empathy framing sidesteps it — the default already fits.

People still use untitled.txt over more powerful tools because customizing has an upfront cost — “I'm busy, I just need to finish.” So will users shape these interfaces or just accept what the AI hands them? We don't fully know. But empathy largely sidesteps it: the whole point is the default already fits, so there's little to tinker with. An empathetic interface does the accommodating — the user shouldn't have to. That's the opposite of untitled.txt, which does nothing until you do everything.

“An interface that knows me that well — what about my privacy?”

A real risk. Local models may keep that context on your own device.

Everything we've proposed depends on the interface knowing your context: your task, your energy budget, that you steer a wheelchair with your head. The empathetic half — the “who is doing this” — needs to know the most about you.

So privacy is an issue. Local models could get us a long way: if the model runs on your own device and the context never leaves your machine, personalization stops being a privacy trade-off. Local models are improving quickly, so we're hopeful rather than certain.

“But a UI that keeps changing breaks screen readers.”

Right: stability is a basic trait of accessibility and of good design in general. Our answer is memory, and least surprise is actually empathy applied. So hopefully this corrects itself with empathetic interfaces.

The sharpest objection: if interfaces are generated, don't you destroy what a screen-reader user relies on — the button being where it was yesterday? Stability is a basic trait of accessibility, and of good design in general. A constantly re-rolling UI would be a disaster.

Our answer is memory. The interface is generated once, for you, then remembered and reused. And an empathetic interface applies exactly this: the least surprise. It knows you navigate by memory, so it holds still — personalize the content, not the structure. So we hope the problem corrects itself: an interface that understands you wouldn't move your controls.

Today's one-size-fits-all UI is the volatile one: it changes under you whenever a vendor redesigns it — that's the Ravelry change that put Rose in bed for three days.

“Does this actually work today?”

Not quite. Models can generate working interfaces; the plumbing around them still fails.

Be honest about where we are. Eight months after our interviews, Rose tried to build a bespoke Steam library manager for herself. It didn't really work out: lack of support, lack of knowledge, still too time-consuming, too much tinkering. Rose is technical and motivated — if anyone could build her own tool, it's her. We replaced configuration toil with developer toil: the AI generated a front end, and what defeated her was the glue — storage, accounts, the plumbing you don't know exists unless you write software.

Is that failure telling us the idea is wrong, or that we're just early? We think it's the moment — and we have evidence, not just hope. Google Research found that generating a working interface is an emergent capability: their newest model produced valid UIs with zero errors, while a model one generation older failed on 60% of prompts. It crossed a threshold rather than improving gradually.

So our honest position: the plumbing that beat Rose is a property of today's tooling, not of the idea. We could be wrong — but we think it's a matter of time, and not much of it.
