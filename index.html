Chatbots and agents are a revolution for accessibility

Simon

So, to give our talk direction, we decided to come up with a hypothesis:

Chatbots and agents are a revolution for accessibility.

Our thought was that: One very simple, and therefore accessible, interface — the natural language chat of these tools — replaces all previous interactions.

No more struggling through websites, apps and content with patchy accessibility support. Actually you can hand over the whole task to the AI, instead of struggling through yourself.

In short: chatbots and agents would remove most of the small and large obstacles people run into every day while relying on screen readers or other assistive technology.

  • To give talk direction: Hypothesis
  • One simple, accessible interface for everything
  • No more patchy a11y support
  • Or just hand the whole task to the AI!

Uses AI as an assistant, not a replacement.

It connects her with volunteers when she needs extra support. She had started using AI for some things, but it couldn’t match the speed and reliability of just doing them herself.

Sarah

With context about you, AI can help you in ways you didn’t anticipate.

“I was really struggling one day … but then, interestingly, it came up with a really insightful reason: it was the drop in barometric pressure. The change had been so fast. And it said that’s really common with people with brain injuries.”

Matt

Next was Sarah. She lives in Canada, so we connected right away! She had a brain injury five years ago, so she came to disability late in life, and she struggled more than Molly and Michael. Screen time was hard, reading a lot in one day was hard, and watching lectures (she was studying for a sailing degree) was hard.

Her whole setup felt like a fight. She had a Chrome plugin that bolds the first half of every word to make reading easier. But it broke her voice control software, which treated each bolded segment as something she could click, so her whole screen filled with numbers over everything. So she switched to a grid overlay instead, narrowing in on an element square by square. But the grid lines were impossible to see once she turned on "Darkify", another Chrome plugin that fakes a dark mode, because her university’s platform didn’t have one. Sometimes she had to switch to Safari for certain tasks, and the whole fragile setup collapsed if she had to watch a lecture video or, God forbid, join a video call.

It was also interesting to hear that she listens better to Gwyneth Paltrow’s voice. She wanted fine control of her tools, but couldn’t figure out how to get it. She actually wanted AI to help her, and was most impressed when it showed it knew more about her illness and situation than she did.

In the quote, she describes a conversation where the agent named the recent weather as the culprit for a particularly difficult day.

No revolution

  • Chatbots & agents: helpful tools
  • The simple interface is very accessible
  • Trust & reliability are the problem
  • We want assistants, not replacements

Simon

At this point in the interviews we regrouped to form an intermediate result. Which was that agents and bots are very helpful tools, but no revolution yet.

The reasons overlap with the general population. They are not accessibility-specific.

Trust and reliability are big hindrances.

People want tools and assistants, they don't want to be replaced. They want to stay in the driver’s seat and “do the work” themselves. Because in the end they are responsible. But also because it is fulfilling.

Myron

An interface where you’re already a power user

“If I had an AI box, I could tell it to line up all my headers from cell A1 to A20 and just send it. That would save so much time. That would be a life changer for me.”

Simon

Our last interview was with Myron, who is a college professor from Louisiana. He has no perception problems — for him it’s purely about movement. He had a spine injury 23 years ago, which left him paralyzed only with head movement.

He steers his wheelchair with head movements, and in Bluetooth mode the same controls also move his mouse pointer.

Watching him work was impressive. He’s very experienced with Dragon NaturallySpeaking for voice control: he knows its shortcuts, makes his own and has memorised where things sit on his screen. To click, he uses the “show grid” command: then nine numbered squares overlay the screen, and each number he says zooms into that specific square, now with a smaller grid. So a sequence like “7, 8, 9, 7” moves the cursor to the precise location of the icon he intended to click.

What slows him down is the number of small steps in one task. Each is its own command: “Show grid. 7, 8, 3. Click. Scroll down. Show grid …”, through every menu. Imagine working on a complicated Excel sheet, this can get tiresome quickly.

Ideally he’d just say what he wants done, and have it done in one go.

The limits of
adaptability

Matt

All our interviewees had adapted their setups to their personal needs, and all of them ran into issues. That brings us to a central problem: the limits of adaptability.

  • Everyone had adapted their setup to their needs
  • And everyone still ran into issues
  • The limits of adaptability

Today’s model

One interface for the average person, with tools and settings on top

…and they start to conflict

Matt

In today’s model we ship one interface.

People either live with it or stack adaptation layers on top.

Click

We saw this in the interviews, where Sarah’s assistive technologies (voice control, dark mode, bionic reading) began to conflict with each other. Each one needs the app to be built in a compatible way, following standards and best practices.

That approach has brought us a long way. In the interviews, it was amazing to see how low the barriers can get. For example, with Myron. But we also saw its limits. Each layer adds complexity to the setup, which means more potential for conflict, more energy spent keeping it working, and more errors.

  • Today we ship one interface: live with it or stack layers on top
  • Sarah: voice control, dark mode and bionic reading fighting each other
  • Every layer needs the app built to standards and best practices
  • That got us far: the barriers can get remarkably low
  • But each layer adds complexity: more conflicts, more upkeep, more errors

One UI doesn’t fit everyone

  • Setups are very personal
  • Adaptations conflict with each other
  • Configuration costs energy
  • Standards have limits

Matt

In fact, one interface trying to fit everyone’s unique needs leads to a unique set of possible errors for each person. Everyone we spoke to had done real work to adapt standard interfaces, but either their setups were too personal, their adaptations were incompatible or poorly maintained, or the configuration was too granular. Or they just lived with the broken experience, because they felt there was nothing more they could do.

Adaptation assumes there’s one interface underneath that everyone bends toward themselves, and that’s where it breaks.

As we saw this again and again, we started to think about a better way. Instead of improving how we adapt the standard interface, we started thinking about how to put these personalizations first, because they matter so much.

  • One interface for unique needs: a unique set of errors for each person
  • Everyone did real work to adapt, and it kept failing
  • Too personal, incompatible, poorly maintained or too granular
  • Or they just lived with the broken experience
  • Adaptation assumes one interface everyone bends toward themselves: that’s where it breaks
  • Not better adaptation, but putting personalization first

A UI generated for your needs.circumstances.state of mind.external environment.needs.

  • your circumstances
  • your state of mind
  • your external environment

Matt

Because in reality, the best interface for a task may need to look different depending on your:

  • needs
  • circumstances
  • state of mind
  • and external environment

So much so that they may not even resemble each other.

This is so important to the final UI that it can’t be a layer on top, or an afterthought. It has to be central, and baked in from the start.

Which means dropping the standard interface altogether, and starting from the person instead.

  • The best interface for a task depends on needs, circumstances, state of mind, surroundings
  • So different they may not resemble each other
  • Not a layer on top or an afterthought: central, baked in from the start
  • Drop the standard interface; start from the person instead

Empathy is
our point

Simon

We call this, maybe a bit grandly, empathy.

  • We call this, maybe a bit grandly, empathy

UI = f(Intent, Empathy)

Intent What you’re trying to do

Empathy Who you are right now

Simon

To take a step back and abstract, for us, a UI should be a function of two elements.

The intent: what you’re trying to do, your current task. And empathy to the user: who you are, your preferences and needs in this very moment.

And from the equation follows directly: Same intent, different person, should result in a different UI.

  • Empathy: the person, their needs and preferences right now

Apps aren’t built for you

Matt

The fundamental barrier is that off-the-shelf apps aren’t built for you. They’re built for the largest group of people they can reach, and anyone outside that group is left out.

They can’t predict every way people will want to use them, and even if they could, building for all of them wouldn’t really be a good business decision.

And generally, we think of this as good design. We make smart compromises, then build MVPs to prove them out. These constraints exist because people are unpredictable, and no team can reasonably account for every way they’ll use an app, let alone do it all well.

So apps take a stance.

For example: Google Maps lets you avoid tolls, highways, and ferries, but not winding roads, if they make you carsick. This user need is left unmet because it would be too complex and the market of users who care is too small and not worth the investment.

  • Off-the-shelf apps can’t predict you
  • We build for the statistical average, the largest demographic
  • We can’t predict every way people will use our apps
  • Meeting every need isn’t economically viable, so we compromise and take a stance
  • Google Maps: avoid tolls, highways and ferries, but not winding roads that make you carsick

The economics changed

“We are excited about a future where users […] have access to an infinite catalog, where the right ephemeral interface is generated on the spot tailored for their need.”
Google Research

Matt

But now, enter AI. Those barriers go away if LLMs can generate a bespoke interface for anyone, however fringe their needs.

Apps already expose APIs, so an LLM can map what they do onto a fresh interface. For the first time, a single-serving interface isn’t just possible: we have an "infinite catalog" of them, to use Google’s words.

We think this will show up alongside and inside existing software. But we’ll also see more small apps that we build entirely ourselves, for exactly what we need. They don’t even need APIs: they can live on the device, have local data, with few outside dependencies.

  • Enter AI: a bespoke interface for anyone, however fringe the need
  • Apps already expose APIs; an LLM puts a fresh interface on top
  • Single-serving interfaces: an "infinite catalog" (Google)
  • Inside existing software, and small apps we build ourselves, even on-device with local data

Chatbots and agents are a revolution for accessibility

Matt

Back to our hypothesis: are chatbots and agents a revolution for accessibility? We think so, but not in the way we first thought.

It’s not that AI becomes an assistant that does the whole task for you.

After our research, we think what’s truly remarkable is that AI makes personalized software development accessible to everyone.

An app you build for yourself naturally knows you best. Rose built a game catalog that respects her disability, and other people are building their own fitness apps, migraine trackers and other interfaces for their own needs.

Of course, software that only one person uses is hard to justify for long. But personalization can extend to friends, families and communities, who build apps and share them among themselves.

  • Back to the hypothesis: a revolution for accessibility, but not as we thought
  • Not an assistant that does the whole task
  • AI makes personalized software development accessible to everyone
  • Rose’s game catalog; others build fitness apps and migraine trackers
  • Hard to justify alone, but it extends to friends, family, community

By the people,
for the people

Matt

I recently listened to Maggie Appleton on the Pragmatic Engineer podcast, and she talks about "barefoot developers". She borrows the term from barefoot doctors: villagers trained in basic medicine to care for their own rural communities, where there were few or no doctors.

Barefoot developers are the same idea for software: people who are technical enough, but aren’t professional programmers, building small, "home-cooked" apps for themselves and the people around them. Problems too local for any company to bother with, solved by someone who empathizes with the people who have them.

That feeling of empowerment used to be reserved for developers. Now, with AI, others feel it too. And that’s exactly what Rose did.

Overall, we believe this democratization of software will be a net benefit.

  • Maggie Appleton: "barefoot developers", borrowed from barefoot doctors in rural villages
  • Technical enough, not professionals: small apps for themselves and the people around them
  • Problems too local for companies, solved by someone who empathizes
  • Empowerment used to be for developers; with AI, others feel it too, like Rose
  • Democratizing software: a net benefit

Imagine everything
is bespoke

  • Lack of privacy
  • Instability
  • Isolation
  • More dependent on AI

Matt

But if everything is bespoke, we get a load of new problems:

  • Privacy: an interface made for you needs to know a lot about you.
  • Stability: how do you get predictable interfaces if they’re made from scratch every time?
  • Isolation: if interfaces shape how we think, what happens to our common ground when we no longer share a visual language?

And one massive drawback: AI keeps becoming a resource-hungry utility we all depend on. That’s probably not good, and we don’t know where it leads. What we do know is that, with AI, personalized interfaces are actually happening for the first time. So now we can think about these issues, discuss them and address them.

  • If everything is bespoke: a load of new problems
  • Privacy: an interface made for you needs to know a lot about you
  • Stability: how are interfaces predictable when made from scratch each time?
  • Isolation: what if we no longer share a visual language?
  • AI as a resource-hungry utility we all depend on
  • Personalized interfaces are happening now, so we can address these issues
